Older generations met a bad year by stocking grain and water. Grain lasts three months; water lasts seven days. Both were only meant to carry you through the worst of it. If the AI transition also counts as a bad year, the thing to stock is not grain. It is a few things that breed on their own.

Before a disaster, look at what you are holding. This time most people hold exactly one thing: an allocation. It is the floor — paid monthly, enough for food, shelter and medical care. You will not starve. You will not get rich.

This piece is about the ceiling: outside that allocation, can an ordinary person still earn something of their own?

The AI era is new to me too. The three items below were worked out while writing. They may be wrong. I am putting them out so that someone will think them through with me.

The first is land. Land, minerals, forests, water — all four share one property: machines cannot make them. AI can copy knowledge at almost zero cost; it cannot copy an acre. There is one Earth, and that number does not grow because compute does.

An inference I am not sure of: once people no longer have to worry about food, birth rates may recover. Today's low fertility is mostly a matter of cost. If it does recover, land and resources only get tighter.

This one has a precondition: it must be a place where land can be privately held. That is no small precondition. In many places it simply does not hold, and where it does not hold, this item is void.

The second is businesses that still earn. Value in the AI era comes out of three places: compute, energy, and the organization that turns those two into products people pay for. None of the three sits with labor. They sit with capital.

For the past century, ordinary people took a share by selling muscle and mind. From here, the share tilts toward capital. However large the pie, if you hold no capital, none of it lands in your bowl.

You will never be Musk, but you can buy his stock. This is not a recommendation. It is a direction: the wage pipe is narrowing, the equity pipe is widening, and a person should move toward the wider one. The good thing about that pipe is that it does not care about size. A thousand dollars gets you in; you do not have to wait until you have a million.

The risk, stated plainly: stocks fall. You buy wrong. You sell on the day you are most frightened. The ordinary person's main opponent is not the market. It is himself.

The third: go where people will go. Once work no longer dictates where you live, why stay somewhere crowded and dirty, where you cannot open a window four months a year? I would choose mild seasons, strong sun, mountains and water. And I would not fuss over proximity to a top-tier hospital. An AI doctor will probably outperform most specialists, and can be copied ten thousand times overnight.

So the question becomes this. Such places are empty now and will be crowded later. If you own a room or a plot there a few years early — a guesthouse, an RV campsite — you catch that wave.

This one differs from the first two. Those are money making money, and you are a spectator. This one is work for money; you have to actually do it. It does not take much capital to start: one room, one plot, one RV counts.

But it is also the easiest to copy. One guesthouse makes money and the village opens ten more the next year. The barrier is not capital. It is judgment, and being early.

One addition: the list does not stop at three. When people are released from work, the first thing they meet is emptiness. Emptiness makes them want something in their hands — painting, an instrument, a craft. Those classes and workshops will not lack students. Open one, teach a few, charge tuition. That is a living too.

But those can be started at any time. Skip this year, do it next year, no harm. So they are not positioning; they are a trade. The first three are different. Land, equity, a good place — all are cheap only while others are still asleep. Once everyone understands, the price already reflects it.

There is much you can do and very little you must do early. The first feeds you. The second is what separates you.

Last, the unpleasant part. Knowing what to stock and being able to stock it are two different things. All of the above, including the classes and workshops, requires spare money first. If you are at the point where the allocation just covers living, all of it is air.

So the real dividing line in the AI era may not be whether you have a job. It may be whether you have a little money left over. The first is visible, and everyone discusses it. The second is quiet, and it decides whether you get a window of your own outside the allocation.
