AI is here. People call it another industrial revolution. I don't quite agree. The steam engine replaced muscle; AI replaces the part of intelligence that can be taught, tested and scored. And that is precisely where education has placed its heaviest bets. So this shock does not arrive with the noise of an industrial revolution. It is more like the light in a classroom going out — quietly, with nobody crying out in pain.

The blow lands on the foundation.

Start with what education actually is. Put bluntly, it is a pricing mechanism: pour a dozen years of knowledge into a person, train him into a machine that solves problems, issue a diploma, and let the diploma convert into a price. The money parents spend and the nights children lose are, in substance, capital paid into an asset called intelligence, in the expectation that it will yield later. We have run that arithmetic for a century without a serious error.

Now there is one.

One example stings: I hear some universities have stopped admitting PhD students in mathematics. A dissertation a doctoral candidate sweats over for months, an AI can flatten in minutes — and in the other direction, the candidate can now finish the dissertation with the AI's help. If even a mathematics dissertation can be flattened like that, other disciplines should at least feel a chill. My reading is that this is likely; only the order of arrival differs.

Which brings up the question nobody wants to ask out loud: from primary school to the college entrance exam — the tutoring fees, the apartment bought for its school district, the ten years of a parent sitting alongside — was any of it worth it?

No one dares answer that straight right now. Mine is: split it in two.

In the short run — and I cannot say how short, that depends on how fast the machines run — a broad education still means something, university still means something, knowing roughly how each field works still means something. But one thing has already lost its price: memorized knowledge, drilled speed, any skill that can be written into a textbook and turned into an exam question. Machines are faster and steadier at all of it. And that is exactly the part the current system drills hardest and examines most finely. A craft practised for a dozen years, and the market suddenly stops wanting it. That is not the child's fault.

What still holds value, I think, is a short list: critical thinking; the capacity for approximation — the nerve to judge and estimate when the information is incomplete; and the human work of organizing, coordinating, persuading. The more precisely machines compute, the more expensive "roughly right" and "holding a group together" become.

I am not arguing for tearing the schools down. Education is the skeleton of a society; replacing bones takes generations, and forcing it only injures the children. But the philosophy ought to shift — toward the IB end of things: more inquiry, more cross-disciplinary work, more speaking and collaborating; less of the arms race of drilling. How far it can actually move is another question, but the direction looks right.

And here is the ugly part. The IB approach costs tuition, costs a certain kind of school, costs parents who can read books themselves. It was never a fair net; it is a sieve, and what it sifts is family resources and range of vision — when the lights go out, who finds a candle first depends on whose drawer you search. Cleverness still has a price; it is the exam transcript that increasingly cannot tell you who is clever. The part schools teach best is going soft, and the part they teach worst is getting more expensive — and the latter is mostly paid for at home. What gets competed on may no longer be scores, but where a child's parents are standing.

Further out, once AGI arrives together with embodied machines that can actually do the work, the question becomes a different one: on what grounds is a person needed then? There is no consensus on that, and I won't pretend to understand it. I'll leave a mark here and trace the outline later.
