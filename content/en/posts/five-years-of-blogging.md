The blog started as a writing exercise. I gave it eighteen months before I would let myself quit. It has now outlived three jobs, two cities, and most of the tools I used to write it.

## I expected writing to improve. Something else did.

- The real gain: I stopped being able to hold a sloppy argument in my head once I had to publish it.
- Writing as a forcing function for thinking — the compression is the point, not the prose.
- The unexpected career effect: three of my last four jobs started with someone reading a post.

## The compounding asset nobody talks about

- Not traffic. Not subscribers. **The archive**: a searchable record of what you actually thought, and when.
- Specific example: a post from year two that answered a question I had forgotten I once understood.
- Why this makes the marginal post cheaper to write than the first one.

## Why most technical blogs die in month four

- Month one is enthusiasm, month two is outlines, month three is the first post nobody reads.
- The metric that predicts survival is not quality — it is whether you have a reason to publish that is not "audience".
- The two blog deaths I have watched up close, and what they had in common.

## The three-post rule

- Publish three posts before touching the design, the domain, or the analytics.
- If you cannot name three things you want to say, the problem is the premise, not the discipline.
- Corollary: never rewrite old posts before you have written ten new ones.

## What I would tell myself at post #1

- Write the post you needed two years ago — the specific one, with the error message in the title.
- Do not wait for the idea to be complete. Publish the part that is true.
- Keep the archive boring and the opinions yours.
