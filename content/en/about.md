Introducing myself is awkward work. The lines on a résumé look like me, but they don't sound like me. What has to be laid on the table is not what I have done — it is how I ended up here.

I entered the best university in the country at fifteen. It sounds like bragging; it was an accident. Not precocity — the examination system happened to fit me the way a key happens to fit a lock. I was young, naive, and surrounded by no one who could point the way. I spent five years walking through fog and came out unable to say what I had learned. Looking back, those five years feel like someone else attended them on my behalf.

The year I graduated, I placed first in the entrance exam for a research institute and went on to graduate school. I had two advisors: the institute's director and the head of its science and technology committee. In the 1980s that arrangement had a name people were slightly embarrassed to say out loud: successor. I took it as my due. When you are young you assume the world will wait — that you can play a while longer and come back for the thing already set aside for you.

A few years later, I left the country.

That was the first rebooking.

I drifted through several places in Europe and North America. The days were livable, even comfortable. Only later did I understand that the word *stability* is a bed for some people and a stone for others — something pressing on your chest until you cannot breathe. Once the idea of working for myself took hold, I could not press it down again. So I packed once more and went home.

That was the second rebooking.

Ten years of building a company; the beatings need no retelling, since anyone who has run one keeps the same ledger. Money was raised, more than once, but money has a way of running out. Watching the cash flow start and stop in a bank account, the worst hours were payroll: by day you talk to people about the future, by night you work out which payment might land first; the phone rings and your chest tightens before you answer. Every pit described in the books, I stepped into carefully; the few the books omitted, I supplied myself.

Later I wound things down and cleared a corner on the internet to hold the thoughts that keep pacing around my head. That was the third rebooking — from the office out there to the one in here.

The ancients spoke of coming to know the mandate of heaven. I first took that as resignation: accept your one road and stop looking around. Later I decided that was wrong. The mandate is not about accepting yourself; it is about looking at the world — which rules are real, which phrases exist to soothe you; and further out, what this universe actually is: where science has got to, where religion has got to, and where the two fail to meet.

This view of mine is probably wrong. A man who talks about where the universe came from on the strength of scattered reading and guesswork deserves to be laughed at. Still, I want to put it out: a wrong thing can sometimes serve as a ladder. It need not be right to let you see your own footing from another angle.

None of this came from sitting still and thinking. When life is comfortable you only want to enjoy it; it is in discomfort that a person starts to think. The few places where I have found some peace were ground out slowly, on bad days. The moment it comes clear is genuinely pleasant — like a lamp switching on in the room. But a lit room is not the same as seeing clearly. Mostly it just casts your own shadow longer.

This corner is where I lay these things out, one at a time — the things I think I have understood. Laid out, they can be judged: which ones hold, which are only my own illusion. If I have got one wrong, come and argue. The footprints went crooked, and I have no intention of straightening them.
