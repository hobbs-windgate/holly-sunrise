Every "first year of indie hacking" post I read before quitting my job had the same shape: a graph going up and to the right, and a paragraph about mindset. None of them told me what the fourth month feels like, or how much of the revenue was actually a refundable annual plan.

This is the version I wish I had read.

## The honest ledger

I kept five columns, and dropping any one of them makes the numbers lie: **month, revenue (gross / after refunds / after payment fees — three separate figures), costs (subscriptions, servers, contractors, tax), actual hours worked, one line of notes.**

By the third month you notice something: the revenue bars and the hours bars move in opposite directions. That is the real shape of the year — not a curve going up and to the right, but a slope where you work more and earn less.

- Define revenue precisely: gross, net of refunds, net of payment fees. The gap is bigger than you think.
- Include the cost nobody counts: your own unpaid hours at a shadow rate.

## What I got wrong about "build in public"

- It is a distribution strategy, not a marketing strategy — it works only if the audience is your customer.
- The posts that drove signups versus the posts I enjoyed writing: almost no overlap.
- What I would publish instead, and what I would stop publishing.

## The cost I underestimated: context switching

- Support, sales, code, and writing all want the same morning.
- The batching rule that recovered ~6 hours a week.
- Why "I will do marketing on Fridays" fails for a one-person company.

## What actually moved the needle

- Talking to ten users who churned — the single highest-return activity of the year.
- Pricing: the change from monthly-only to annual-with-discount.
- The one integration that brought most of the traffic.

## What I would do differently

- Start with a paid pilot instead of a free beta.
- Pick a niche narrow enough to be the obvious answer.
- Decide the "quit condition" before month one, in writing.
